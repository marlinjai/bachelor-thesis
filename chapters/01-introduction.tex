\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

A carbon price applied to domestic producers but not to their foreign
competitors creates an obvious incentive: buy the emissions-intensive input
abroad. Whether that incentive is large enough to matter has been contested for
two decades. Ex-ante simulation models place the leakage rate for unilateral
climate policy somewhere between five and thirty percent
\parencite{branger2014}, while the ex-post evidence on the \acrfull{ets} has
generally failed to find the relocation that the models predict
\parencite{martin2016,naegele2019}. The gap between the two literatures is
usually attributed to \gls{freeallocation}: European installations in exposed
sectors have received most of their allowances at no charge, so the marginal
incentive to relocate has been muted by design.

That arrangement is now being dismantled. Regulation (EU) 2023/956
\parencite{eu2023cbam} establishes the \acrfull{cbam}, under which importers of
covered goods must surrender certificates priced against the \gls{ets}, while
free allocation to the corresponding domestic sectors is withdrawn on a
matching schedule. The mechanism ran in a reporting-only transitional phase
from October 2023, and the definitive regime --- with an actual financial
obligation attached --- began on 1 January 2026.

This is an unusually clean setting. The date was not chosen by any importer.
Coverage is defined not by a discretionary judgement about which firms are
exposed, but by an explicit list of \acrfull{cn} codes in the regulation's
annex. Immediately adjacent to that list sit products that are physically and
commercially similar but fall outside scope. The treated set, in other words,
writes itself, and a plausible control group is available at the same level of
aggregation.

\section{Research Question}
\label{sec:research-question}

The thesis asks one question and decomposes it into three:

\begin{quote}
\textit{Did the start of the definitive \acrshort{cbam} regime change what
Europe imports, from whom, and at what price?}
\end{quote}

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1 (Volume)] Did import volumes of covered \acrshort{cn} codes fall
    relative to uncovered near-neighbour codes after January 2026?
  \item[RQ2 (Reallocation)] Did import shares shift from
    high-carbon-intensity origins towards low-carbon-intensity origins, and is
    any such shift consistent with real reallocation rather than
    \gls{resourceshuffling}?
  \item[RQ3 (Incidence)] Did \glspl{unitvalue} of covered goods rise, and does
    the increase approximate the certificate cost --- indicating pass-through
    onto European buyers --- or fall short of it, indicating absorption by
    non-EU exporters?
\end{description}

RQ3 is where the policy interest concentrates. A border adjustment that is
fully absorbed abroad protects domestic industry at foreign expense; one that
is fully passed through is a consumption tax on European manufacturers. The
distinction is not visible in aggregate trade statistics without the kind of
control group this design supplies.

\section{Contribution}
\label{sec:contribution}

Three contributions are intended. First, the thesis provides quasi-experimental
estimates of the definitive \acrshort{cbam} regime rather than simulations of
it; to the author's knowledge no such estimates yet exist, for the simple
reason that the regime is less than a year old. Second, it separates the levy
from the simultaneous free-allocation phase-out. Policy commentary routinely
treats these as one intervention, but they act on opposite sides of the border
and their effects on import volumes have opposite signs. Third, it documents a
scope-boundary identification strategy that remains valid as later phases
extend coverage to further \acrshort{cn} codes, so the design can be re-run
rather than rebuilt.

\section{Scope and Limitations}
\label{sec:scope}

Two limitations are structural and are stated here rather than buried in
\Cref{ch:discussion}. The post-treatment window is short: Comext publishes with
a lag of roughly two months, so at the time of writing the analysis observes
the first two to three quarters of 2026. Estimates should therefore be read as
short-run responses, and long-run supply-chain reorganisation is out of reach.
Second, the design identifies the effect of \acrshort{cbam} \emph{relative to
near-neighbour products}. If the mechanism moved economy-wide expectations ---
plausible, given how much anticipatory commentary preceded it --- the control
group is contaminated and the estimates are a lower bound.
\Cref{sec:threats} takes this seriously rather than noting it in passing.
