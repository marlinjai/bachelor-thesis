\section{Constructing Treatment and Control}
\label{sec:treatment-control}

The treated set is every \acrshort{cn}-8 code listed in Annex~I of the
regulation. Control codes are selected by a rule fixed before any outcome is
inspected, so that the comparison set is not chosen to produce a result:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Proximity.} The candidate shares the first six \acrshort{hs}
    digits with a treated code, or sits in the same four-digit heading where no
    six-digit neighbour exists.
  \item \textbf{Exclusion.} The candidate does not itself appear in Annex~I,
    and is not scheduled for inclusion in a published later phase.
  \item \textbf{Non-substitutability.} The candidate is not a plausible
    demand-side substitute for its treated neighbour. This is the binding
    criterion and the one requiring judgement; \Cref{sec:substitution} sets
    out how it is applied and how the resulting classification is stress-tested.
  \item \textbf{Support.} Both codes record non-trivial import flows in every
    month of the pre-period, so that the pair contributes to identification
    rather than to noise.
\end{enumerate}

Criterion~3 deserves emphasis. A control that importers can switch to is
contaminated by the treatment, and the resulting estimate overstates the effect
by counting the substitution twice --- once as a fall in the treated code and
once as a rise in the control. Where substitutability is genuinely ambiguous,
the pair is dropped from the main sample and reintroduced in a robustness
specification, so the cost of the judgement is visible.

\section{Sample Period}
\label{sec:sample-period}

The estimation window runs from January 2021 to the most recent month available
at the time of writing. Three sub-periods are distinguished, and conflating
them would be a mistake:

\begin{description}
  \item[Pre-period (2021--Sep 2023)] No \acrshort{cbam} obligation of any kind.
    Supplies the pre-trends.
  \item[Transitional (Oct 2023--Dec 2025)] Reporting only. Anticipatory
    adjustment is possible here, which is why this window is estimated as its
    own event-time coefficient rather than pooled into the pre-period.
  \item[Definitive (Jan 2026--)] Certificate obligation in force. The
    treatment window.
\end{description}

Treating the transitional phase as untreated would attribute any anticipation
to the pre-trend and bias the estimate towards zero. Treating it as treated
would confuse a paperwork requirement with a price. It is therefore estimated
separately, and the difference between the two coefficients is itself
informative about whether firms respond to announced or to realised costs.

\section{Known Data Problems}
\label{sec:data-problems}

\textbf{Confidentiality suppression.} Eurostat suppresses cells that would
disclose an individual trader. Suppression is non-random --- it correlates with
concentrated origins, which are exactly the ones of interest --- so suppressed
cells are tracked and reported rather than dropped silently.

\textbf{Unit-value noise.} \Glspl{unitvalue} are value over quantity and
therefore inherit every quantity-reporting error. Extreme values are winsorised
at the first and ninety-ninth percentile within \acrshort{cn} code, and the
threshold is varied in robustness checks.

\textbf{Short post-period.} With roughly two to three quarters observed, the
dynamic specification has few post-treatment event-time coefficients. This
limits what can be said about persistence, and \Cref{ch:discussion} does not
say more than the data supports.

\textbf{The 2022 energy shock.} Energy prices moved European industrial input
costs violently in the pre-period, and did so differentially across
energy-intensive sectors --- which is to say, differentially between treated
and control. This is the most serious threat to parallel trends in the sample
and is addressed directly in \Cref{sec:threats}.
