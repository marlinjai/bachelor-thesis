\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

On 1 January 2026 the European Union's \acrfull{cbam} moved from a
reporting-only transitional phase to a definitive regime in which importers
must surrender certificates against the \gls{embeddedemissions} of covered
goods. This thesis asks whether that switch measurably redirected European
sourcing.

The identification strategy exploits the fact that coverage is defined by an
explicit list of \acrshort{cn} codes. Covered products are compared against
near-neighbour codes that sit just outside the annex --- similar goods, similar
buyers, similar cyclical exposure, but no certificate obligation --- in a
\acrfull{did} design on monthly Eurostat Comext import records. Three outcomes
are examined: import volumes, the reallocation of import shares across origin
countries of differing carbon intensity, and \glspl{unitvalue} as a proxy for
price pass-through.

[Results to be completed once the estimation is run.] The analysis is designed
to distinguish genuine emissions reallocation from \gls{resourceshuffling}, and
to report how much of any measured effect survives the honest-parallel-trends
sensitivity analysis of \textcite{rambachan2023}.

The contribution is threefold: it provides the first quasi-experimental
estimates of the definitive \acrshort{cbam} regime; it separates the levy's
effect from the simultaneous phase-out of \gls{freeallocation}, which most
policy commentary conflates; and it documents an identification strategy that
remains usable as later phases widen the product scope.
