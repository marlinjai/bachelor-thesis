\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
\selectlanguage{english}

The waste hierarchy places the reuse of a component above its material
recovery. Yet a reclaimed timber beam put back into load-bearing service must
be assigned a strength, and the applicable rules assess only visible features
and density. None of them captures what decades under load have done to the
wood.

A beam in bending carries compressive stress above the neutral axis and
tensile stress below it, and timber fails differently in these two
directions: in a brittle manner through crack growth under tension, and in a
ductile manner through cell wall buckling under compression. This thesis
therefore asks whether the residual strength of a used beam differs between
its former compression zone and its former tension zone.

Drawing on a review of waste and standards law, the state of research and the
material physics of load history, a hypothesis is derived and a test design
proposed: paired small clear specimens from both zones of the same beam,
density as a covariate, and strict exclusion of any beam whose original
orientation cannot be established. The review shows that previous studies
treat reclaimed timber as a whole, and that the available non-destructive
methods yield readings integrated over the cross section, which systematically
conceal any difference between the zones.

The experiments remain to be carried out. The contribution is the theoretical
groundwork and the test design, not measured results.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} reclaimed timber, reuse, residual strength, load
history, compression zone, tension zone, circular economy
